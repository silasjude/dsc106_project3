Exploring vegetation and temperature changes in the Amazon

When the Amazon rainforest is cut down, the ground underneath gets hotter. Twenty-two years of NASA satellite data show how much.

A rainforest works like an evaporative cooler. Trees pull water from the soil and breathe it out through their leaves, and that evaporation carries heat away from the ground. Pasture and cropland move much less water, so more of the sun's energy goes into heating the surface.

NASA's MODIS instrument on the Terra satellite has measured both sides of this since 2000: how green the land is (NDVI) and how hot the land surface is at about 10:30 am local time (land surface temperature, LST). Below, each 5 km square of the southern Amazon carries both numbers for every dry season from 2001 to 2022.

Our question: does losing forest cover make the land surface measurably hotter, and by how much?

How the numbers were made

  1. Source. NASA MODIS on the Terra satellite: monthly NDVI (~1 km) and monthly daytime land surface temperature (~5 km), July–September of every year, downloaded through NASA GIBS and decoded from color back to values with each layer's published colormap.
  2. Grid. 75°W–45°W, 18°S–2°N, cut into 0.05° squares (about 5.5 km). A square's NDVI is the mean of the ~25 one-km pixels inside it.
  3. Dry season. One value per square per year: the median of the July, August and September composites. The dry season has the fewest clouds, and it is when pasture and forest differ most.
  4. Change. Each year minus that square's own 2001–2003 average. The map and charts show a 3-year average ending in the selected year, which smooths out single drought years (2005, 2010, 2015–16).
  5. Cleared / stable. A square counts as cleared when its greenness fell more than 0.20 below baseline, and stable when it moved less than 0.05 either way. On the "Year cleared" map, a square is cleared in the first year (2004 or later) its NDVI dropped 0.20 below baseline and stayed there for 3 years.
  6. Clearing effect. Average temperature change of cleared squares minus that of stable squares in the same area. Regional warming and drought hit both groups, so subtracting cancels them out and leaves what clearing added.
  7. Why stop at 2022. Terra's orbit has been drifting since 2022, so it now passes over earlier in the morning, when the ground is cooler. Untouched forest in our control box "cooled" by 1–2 °C in 2025–26 for that reason alone, so later years would mislead.

Limits. 5 km squares blur small clearings, so cleared area here is a lower bound. A drop in NDVI can also come from fire or drought (the 3-year persistence rule and the stable-square comparison limit this but can't remove it). LST is a skin temperature at one time of day, not air temperature.
