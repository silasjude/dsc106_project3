Write-up

Exploring vegetation and temperature changes in the Amazon · DSC 106 Project 3 · Team 11

Motivation

Our checkpoint question was whether deforestation in the Amazon shows up as a change in temperature. A forest cools the ground by moving water from the soil into the air, and pasture moves much less water, so more sunlight ends up as heat. We wanted readers to be able to check that claim themselves on real measurements, place by place and year by year, and to come away with a number. In our data, land that was cleared ended up roughly 4 °C hotter in the dry season than forest next to it that was left standing.

We started the project with global maps of several MODIS products, narrowed to vegetation and land surface temperature, then to the southern Amazon, and finally to the comparison between cleared and stable land that the page is built around.

Design rationale

Data transformations

  • Decoding. NASA GIBS serves coloured images, not numbers. We matched every pixel colour back to a value with each layer's published colour map. NDVI was requested at 0.01° and averaged into 0.05° squares (about 5.5 km) so it sits on the same grid as temperature.
  • Dry season only. Each year is the median of July, August and September. Forest stays green in the dry season and pasture dries out, so the contrast is largest then, and the wet season is too cloudy to use. A median of three months survives one bad month.
  • Change, not level. Everything is shown as the difference from the same square's 2001–03 average. Raw temperature mostly reflects elevation and latitude, and subtracting each square's own baseline removes that.
  • Three-year averages. The map shows the three years ending at the selected year, because single years swing with drought and smoke (2005, 2010, 2015–16).
  • Cleared and stable. A square is cleared when its NDVI is more than 0.20 below baseline, and stable when it is within 0.05. We picked 0.20 because almost no squares in our untouched control box fall that far, so it sits outside normal noise.
  • Clearing effect. The headline number is the average temperature change of cleared squares minus that of stable squares in the same area. Regional warming and drought hit both groups, so the subtraction leaves what clearing added.
  • Stopping at 2022. Terra's orbit has drifted since then, so it passes over earlier in the morning and reads cooler. Our checkpoint plot of the Sahara showed the same artificial drop, so we cut the record there.

These steps are also listed on the main page under “How the numbers were made”, and the map's subtitle says which ones apply to the current view.

Visual encodings

  • Map. Position is geography, and each 5 km square is one pixel. Change has a direction and a real zero, so both change layers use a diverging colour scale with a pale middle: brown to teal for greenness (brown reads as bare ground) and blue to red for temperature. The scales end at ±0.3 NDVI and ±5 °C so that a few extreme squares do not wash out everything else. Water and missing data are a dark grey that is on neither scale.
  • One map, two layers. Greenness and temperature use the same grid, so toggling between them keeps every square in place and the eye can match the brown patches to the red ones.
  • Readout. The answer to our question is a single number per area (cleared minus stable warming), so it is shown as a large number beside the map and updates with the region and year.
  • Dark background. Against black, the map is the brightest thing on screen, which is where we want the eye.

Interaction techniques

  • Year slider and play button. Dragging the slider or pressing play steps through the years, which shows clearing spreading out from the roads and the heat following it. A single before-and-after map hides that.
  • Layer toggle switches the map between greenness change and temperature change, so the reader can see that the places that lost the most green are the places that got hottest.
  • Region buttons pick the area the readout summarizes, and the map zooms in on it so the reader can see the individual cleared patches. Choosing "Whole map" zooms back out. The intact control is the important one, because it shows how little happens where nothing was cut.
  • Hover averages the squares inside a small circle (about 15 km across) that follows the pointer, and shows the share of that area cleared since 2004 followed by its greenness and temperature change. Single 5 km squares were too precise to aim at and flickered with every small mouse movement; larger fixed blocks were hard to read.

Alternatives we considered

  • Three separate designs. Each of three of us built a full version on a branch: this one, a version with two maps side by side and a bar-chart year slider, and a lighter version with a one-direction scatter brush. We merged them, keeping the first as the base and taking the state outlines and the Mato Grosso region from the third.
  • A larger multi-view dashboard. Our merged build also had a brushable greenness-vs-temperature scatter, a cleared-vs-stable time series, a "year cleared" map layer, drag-to-select on the map, a guided tour and a before/after satellite photo. The assignment asks for one focused graphic, so we cut back to the map and kept only the interactions that serve our question.
  • Two maps side by side. Easy to compare at a glance, but each map is half the size. We kept one large map with a toggle.
  • A bar-chart year slider. An earlier build used a bar chart of newly cleared land as the slider. It added information, but it was a second chart to read, so we went back to a plain slider.
  • A light page. Our first builds were light. The dark version won when we put them next to each other.
  • One dual-axis chart of NDVI and temperature. Rejected, because the apparent relationship would depend on how we scaled the two axes.
  • Extending to 2026. Rejected after we found the orbit drift.

Limits

A drop in NDVI is a sign of clearing, not proof, since fire and drought lower it too. Squares are 5 km wide, so small clearings are diluted and our cleared area is a lower bound. Values arrive from GIBS already rounded into colour-map bins (0.6 °C for temperature). The temperature is that of the surface at about 10:30 in the morning, not the air temperature a weather station would report. And this is observational data: it is consistent with clearing causing the warming, but it does not prove it.

Development process

How the work was split

  • Ethan Lam wrote the first exploration notebook, including the code that turns GIBS images back into numbers, which everything after it reuses.
  • Silas Jude made the global exploratory figures, set up the repository and GitHub Pages, and built the version with linked maps and the bar-chart year slider.
  • McAllister Blair wrote the deforestation notebook that narrowed the topic to the Amazon, and built “The Heat Left Behind”, which is the base of the final page.
  • Rami Abukhater built the third version and merged the three into the final submission.

Time spent

About 18 people-hours in total: roughly 5 hours each for Silas, Ethan and McAllister, and 3 hours for Rami.

What took the most time

Most of the time went into decoding GIBS images, finding out that temperatures after 2022 are not comparable, and choosing thresholds so that the control box shows almost no clearing. In the page itself, the slow parts were keeping 240,000 squares redrawing smoothly as the year changes (the map is a canvas, and only the overlays are SVG), and then reconciling three finished designs into one and cutting it back to a single effective graphic.
