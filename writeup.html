Write-up

Exploring vegetation and temperature changes in the Amazon · DSC 106 Project 3 · Team 11

Motivation

Our checkpoint question was whether deforestation in the Amazon shows up as a change in temperature. A forest cools the ground by moving water from the soil into the air, and pasture moves much less water, so more sunlight ends up as heat. We wanted readers to be able to check that claim themselves on real measurements, place by place and year by year, and to come away with a number. In our data, land that was cleared ended up roughly 4 °C hotter in the dry season than forest next to it that was left standing.

We started the project with global maps of several MODIS products, narrowed to vegetation and land surface temperature, then to the southern Amazon, and finally to the comparison between cleared and stable land that the page is built around.

Design rationale

Data transformations

  • Decoding. NASA GIBS serves coloured images, not numbers. We matched every pixel colour back to a value with each layer's published colour map. NDVI was requested at 0.01° and averaged into 0.05° squares (about 5.5 km) so it sits on the same grid as temperature.
  • Dry season only. Each year is the median of July, August and September. Forest stays green in the dry season and pasture dries out, so the contrast is largest then, and the wet season is too cloudy to use. A median of three months survives one bad month.
  • Change, not level. Everything is shown as the difference from the same square's 2001–03 average. Raw temperature mostly reflects elevation and latitude, and subtracting each square's own baseline removes that.
  • Three-year averages. The map and scatter show the three years ending at the selected year, because single years swing with drought and smoke (2005, 2010, 2015–16).
  • Cleared and stable. A square is cleared when its NDVI is more than 0.20 below baseline, and stable when it is within 0.05. We picked 0.20 because almost no squares in our untouched control box fall that far, so it sits outside normal noise. For the “year cleared” map the drop also has to last three years.
  • Clearing effect. The headline number is the average temperature change of cleared squares minus that of stable squares in the same area. Regional warming and drought hit both groups, so the subtraction leaves what clearing added.
  • Stopping at 2022. Terra's orbit has drifted since then, so it passes over earlier in the morning and reads cooler. Our checkpoint plot of the Sahara showed the same artificial drop, so we cut the record there.

These steps are also listed on the main page under “How the numbers were made”, and each chart's subtitle says which ones apply to it.

Visual encodings

  • Map. Position is geography, and each 5 km square is one pixel. Change has a direction and a real zero, so both change layers use a diverging colour scale with a pale middle: brown to teal for greenness (brown reads as bare ground) and blue to red for temperature. The scales end at ±0.3 NDVI and ±5 °C so that a few extreme squares do not wash out everything else. Water and missing data are a dark grey that is on neither scale.
  • Year cleared. Year is an ordered quantity, so it gets a single ramp from deep orange (2004) to pale yellow (2022). On the dark page this makes the newest clearing the brightest thing on the map.
  • Scatter. The relationship between the two measures is the answer to our question, so it gets position on both axes. About 230,000 points would overplot into a blob, so we bin them into rectangles and colour by count on a log scale, which keeps the thin tail of heavily cleared squares visible next to the huge pile near zero. The white line is the median temperature change in each slice of greenness change, and the bracket marks the clearing effect directly on the chart.
  • Time series. Cleared, stable and the intact control share one °C axis and are labelled at the line ends, so colour is not the only cue. The share of land cleared has different units, so it has its own small chart under the lines. We did not want a dual axis.
  • Black background. The header photo is dark satellite imagery, and we carried that through the whole page. Against black, the map and the lit squares in a selection are the brightest things on screen, which is where we want the eye. We rechecked every chart colour for contrast after the switch; the cleared and stable lines became a lighter coral and teal.

Interaction techniques

  • Year slider that is also a chart. The slider is a bar chart of how much land was newly cleared each year in the selected area. It shows when the clearing happened before you drag, and dragging or pressing play shows it spreading out from the roads, which a single before-and-after map hides.
  • Brushing the map filters the scatter, the lines, the readout and the slider's bars to that area. This is how a reader tests the claim locally: a clearing frontier and an untouched block give visibly different pictures.
  • Brushing the scatter goes the other way and shows where a part of the distribution lives. Box the hot, cleared corner and only those squares stay lit on the map, strung along the highways. A static figure can show that the two measures are correlated, but not this.
  • Hover gives one square's 22-year history of greenness and temperature, including the year it was cleared.
  • Presets jump to the areas from our analysis. The intact control is the important one, because it shows how little happens where nothing was cut.
  • The tour sets up the view for each finding in order, and the reader can leave it at any step.
  • The satellite swipe at the top is a hook: two real photographs of Rondônia twenty years apart, before any abstract data.

Alternatives we considered

  • Three separate designs. Each of three of us built a full version on a branch: this one, a version with two maps side by side and a bar-chart year slider, and a lighter version with a one-direction scatter brush. The final page keeps the first as the base and takes the slider from the second and the state outlines and extra preset from the third.
  • Two maps side by side. Easy to compare at a glance, but each map is half the size, and the scatter does the same comparison more precisely. We kept one large map with a toggle.
  • A plain range slider. It works, but it tells you nothing until you move it.
  • A plain point scatter, or hexagonal bins. Points are unreadable at this size. Hexagons need a plugin outside core D3, and rectangles line up with the axis ticks and the brush edges.
  • A light page. Our first builds were light. The dark version won when we put them next to each other.
  • One dual-axis chart of NDVI and temperature. Rejected, because the apparent relationship would depend on how we scaled the two axes.
  • Extending to 2026. Rejected after we found the orbit drift.

Limits

A drop in NDVI is a sign of clearing, not proof, since fire and drought lower it too. Squares are 5 km wide, so small clearings are diluted and our cleared area is a lower bound. Values arrive from GIBS already rounded into colour-map bins (0.6 °C for temperature). The temperature is that of the surface at about 10:30 in the morning, not the air temperature a weather station would report. And this is observational data: it is consistent with clearing causing the warming, but it does not prove it.

Development process

How the work was split

  • Ethan Lam wrote the first exploration notebook, including the code that turns GIBS images back into numbers, which everything after it reuses.
  • Silas Jude made the global exploratory figures, set up the repository and GitHub Pages, and built the version with linked maps and the bar-chart year slider.
  • McAllister Blair wrote the deforestation notebook that narrowed the topic to the Amazon, and built “The Heat Left Behind”, which is the base of the final page.
  • Rami Abukhater built the third version and merged the three into the final submission.

Time spent

About 18 people-hours in total: roughly 5 hours each for Silas, Ethan and McAllister, and 3 hours for Rami.

What took the most time

Not the drawing. Most of the time went into getting numbers we could trust: decoding GIBS images, finding out that temperatures after 2022 are not comparable, and choosing thresholds so that the control box shows almost no clearing. In the page itself, the slow parts were the two-way link between the map and the scatter, keeping 240,000 squares redrawing smoothly while a brush is dragged (the map is a canvas, and only the overlays are SVG), and then reconciling three finished designs into one.
