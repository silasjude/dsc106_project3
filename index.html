Where the Amazon was cleared, the ground got hotter

Does cutting down rainforest change the temperature of the land underneath it? Two NASA MODIS measurements, vegetation greenness (NDVI) and daytime land surface temperature (LST), let you check for yourself, 5 km at a time, from 2001 to 2022.

What to look for

Start with the whole map and flip between Greenness change and Temperature change. The brown arc along the southern and eastern edge of the forest (Rondônia, northern Mato Grosso, Pará) is the same shape as the red arc on the temperature map. Then drag across the left side of the scatter plot, where greenness fell the most. The map keeps only those cells, and with the temperature map switched on almost all of them are red. Last, jump to Intact interior: the scatter collapses to a small blob near zero. Forest that was left alone barely moved on either axis, which is why we read the warming as a result of clearing and not of the whole region heating up.

Design rationale

The question and the data

Our checkpoint question was whether deforestation in the Amazon shows up as a temperature change. We use two monthly MODIS Terra products from NASA GIBS: NDVI, which measures how green the surface is, and daytime land surface temperature. The map covers 75°W–45°W and 18°S–2°N on a 0.05° grid (about 5.5 km), which is the native grid of the temperature product. That is 240,000 cells for each of 22 years.

Transformations

  • Dry season only. Each year is the median of July, August and September. Forest stays green in the dry season and pasture dries out, so the contrast is largest then. The wet season is also too cloudy to give usable data. A median of three months survives one bad month.
  • Change, not level. Every view shows the difference from a 2001–03 baseline. Raw temperature is dominated by elevation and latitude (the Andes are cold whatever happens to the forest), and subtracting each cell's own baseline removes that.
  • Three-year windows. The map and scatter average the three years ending at the slider year. Single years swing a lot with drought and smoke. The line chart is the one place that keeps single years, so the noise is visible and not hidden.
  • Forest filter. The charts only count cells that were dense forest at the start (baseline dry-season NDVI of 0.7 or more). Without this, the savanna and farmland south of the forest mix a different story into the scatter. Those cells are still on the map, faded, so nothing disappears silently.
  • “Cleared” and “stayed forest”. A cell counts as cleared if its NDVI dropped by 0.2 or more by 2020–22, and as intact if it moved by less than 0.05. The 0.2 cut-off comes from our exploration notebook: in a remote control region almost no cells fall that far, so it sits outside normal noise.
  • Stopping at 2022. Terra's orbit has been drifting since then, so it passes over later in the day and its temperatures are no longer comparable. Our checkpoint plot of the Sahara showed the same artificial drop after 2023, so we cut the series there.

Visual encodings

  • Map: position for place, diverging colour for change. Change has a direction and a meaningful zero, so both measures use a two-ended colour scale with a pale middle. Greenness runs brown to teal and temperature runs blue to red. Both are standard ColorBrewer schemes that stay readable with colour-blindness, and we kept them different so it is always obvious which measure is showing. Missing data is a flat grey that appears on neither scale, so it cannot be read as “no change”.
  • Scatter: position for both variables. The relationship between the two measures is the actual answer to the question, so it gets the most accurate channel on both axes. With more than 150,000 forest cells a plain scatter is a solid blob, so we bin the cells into a grid and colour each rectangle by how many cells fall in it, on a log scale so the sparse tail of heavily cleared cells is still visible next to the huge pile of unchanged ones. The black line is the median temperature change in each 0.05-wide slice of greenness change. It is there because the eye is bad at judging a trend from density alone.
  • Lines: a shared baseline. Both groups are plotted as change from their own 2001–03 average, so they start together at zero and the reader only has to watch the gap. The two lines are labelled directly at their ends, and orange against blue is safe for red-green colour-blindness.

Interactions

  • Brushing the map filters both charts to that area. This is what lets you test the claim locally: a clearing frontier and an untouched block give visibly different scatters, which a single static scatter of the whole basin would average away.
  • Brushing the scatter goes the other way and shows where a part of the distribution lives. This is the interaction we think earns its place the most. A static figure can show that greenness loss and warming are correlated, but not that the cells in the top-left corner are all on the roads through Rondônia and Pará.
  • The year slider and play button show the order of events: the brown arc spreads first along the highways, and the scatter's tail stretches up and to the left as it does.
  • The measure toggle swaps the map between the two variables without changing anything else, so the eye can compare the same place.
  • Hovering gives the real numbers for one cell and marks it on the scatter, which ties one place on the ground to one point in the cloud.

Alternatives we considered

  • Two maps side by side instead of a toggle. This is what our checkpoint figure did. It makes the patterns easy to compare at a glance, but each map ends up half the size, and the comparison it supports is done more precisely by the scatter. We kept one large map.
  • A bivariate colour map that mixes both measures into one colour. It needs a nine-colour legend that most readers cannot decode, so we dropped it.
  • Pan and zoom on the map. We left it out. At 5 km per cell there is no extra detail to zoom into, and dragging to pan would compete with dragging to select. The area presets cover the same need.
  • Hexagonal bins, as in our exploration plot. Rectangles line up with the axis ticks and with the brush edges, which makes “everything left of −0.2” an exact selection.
  • A “year the forest was cleared” map from the exploration notebook. It is a good figure, but it answers when, not what happened to temperature, so it would have been a second project.

Limits

A drop in NDVI is a sign of clearing, not proof. Fire and drought lower it too. Cells are 5 km wide, so small clearings are diluted. NDVI values from GIBS arrive already rounded into colour-map bins, so differences under about 0.01 mean nothing. Temperature arrives the same way, in steps of 0.6 °C, which is fine for a 4 °C signal but not for fine detail. And the temperature is the temperature of the surface at about 10:30 in the morning, not the air temperature a weather station would report.

The part that took longest was not drawing anything. In the interactive itself, most of the time went into the two-way link between the map and the scatter, and into keeping 240,000 cells redrawing smoothly while a brush is being dragged, which is why the map is drawn on a canvas and only the overlays are SVG.
