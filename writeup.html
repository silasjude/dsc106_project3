Write-up

Exploring vegetation and temperature changes in the Amazon · DSC 106 Project 3 · Team 11

Motivation

Our checkpoint question was whether deforestation in the Amazon shows up as a change in temperature. A forest cools the ground by moving water from the soil into the air, and pasture moves much less water, so more sunlight ends up as heat. We wanted readers to be able to check that claim themselves on real measurements, place by place and year by year, and to come away with a number. In our data, land that was cleared ended up roughly 4 °C hotter in the dry season than forest next to it that was left standing.

We started the project with global maps of several MODIS products, narrowed to vegetation and land surface temperature, then to the southern Amazon, and finally to the comparison between cleared and stable land that the page is built around.

Design rationale

Data transformations

  • Decoding. The layers come as colored pixels, so we used each layer's published color map to turn every pixel back into an actual value. We pulled NDVI at 0.01° and then averaged it up into 0.05° squares (roughly 5.5 km) so it lines up with the temperature grid.
  • Dry season only. Each year is the median of July, August and September. In the dry season forest stays green while pasture dries out, so that's when the difference between them is biggest. The wet season is also way too cloudy to use. Using the median of three months means outliers don't throw off the whole year.
  • Change rather than Value. Everything is shown as how much a square changed from its own 2001–03 average. Raw temperature mostly just reflects elevation and latitude, so subtracting each square's own baseline gets rid of that.
  • Three-year averages. The map shows the average of the three years ending at whatever year you pick. Single years jump around a lot because of drought and smoke (2005, 2010, 2015, 2016), so averaging smooths that out.
  • Cleared and stable. We call a square cleared if its NDVI dropped more than 0.20 below its baseline, and stable if it stayed within 0.05. We went with 0.20 because almost none of the squares in our untouched control box ever drop that far, so anything past it isn't just normal noise.
  • Clearing effect. The main number is the average temperature change in cleared squares minus the average in stable squares from the same area. Regional warming and drought hit both groups, so subtracting one from the other leaves just the relative part that can be more easily attributed to clearing.
  • Stopping at 2022. After 2022, Terra's orbit drifted enough that it passes over earlier in the morning, which makes temperatures read cooler than they really are. Our Sahara checkpoint plot showed the same fake drop, so we cut the data off there.

Visual encodings

  • Map. Position is geography, and each 5 km square is one pixel. Change has a direction and a zero, so both change layers use a diverging colour scale with a pale middle: brown to teal for greenness (brown reads as bare ground) and blue to red for temperature. Both are fairly standard and easily interpretable. The scales end at ±0.3 NDVI and ±5 °C so that individual extreme outliers don't wash out everything else. Water and missing data are a dark grey that is on neither scale.
  • Two lauers. Greenness and temperature use the same grid, so toggling between them keeps every square in place and the eye can match the brown patches to the red ones.
  • Readout. The answer to our question is a single number per area (cleared minus stable warming), so it is shown as a large number beside the map and updates with the region and year.
  • Dark background. Against black, the map is the brightest thing on screen, which is where we want the eye.

Interaction techniques

  • Year slider and play button. Dragging the slider or pressing play steps through the years, which shows clearing spreading out from the roads and the heat following it. This couldn't be shown as effectively with just a before/after.
  • Layer toggle switches the map between greenness change and temperature change, so the reader can see that the places that lost the most green are the places that got hottest.
  • Region buttons pick the area the readout summarizes, and the map zooms in on it so the reader can see the individual cleared patches. Choosing "Whole map" zooms back out.
  • Hover averages the squares inside a small circle (about 15 km across) that follows the pointer, and shows the share of that area cleared since 2004 followed by its greenness and temperature change.

Alternatives we considered

  • Alternative designs. Three of us built our own baseline versions on seperate branches: the predecessor to this one, a version with two maps side by side and a bar-chart year slider, and a lighter version with a one-direction scatter brush. We merged them, keeping the first as the base and taking the state outlines and the Mato Grosso region from the third.
  • A larger multi-view dashboard. Our merged build also had a brushable greenness-vs-temperature scatter, a cleared-vs-stable time series, a "year cleared" map layer, drag-to-select on the map, a guided tour and a before/after satellite photo. The assignment asks for one focused graphic, so we cut back to the map and kept only the interactions that serve our question.
  • Two maps side by side. Easy to compare at a glance, but each map is half the size. We kept one large map with a toggle.
  • A bar-chart year slider. An earlier build used a bar chart of newly cleared land as the slider. It added information, but it was a second chart to read, so we went back to a plain slider.
  • A light page. Our first builds were light, but we ultimately preferred the dark background.
  • One dual-axis chart of NDVI and temperature. Rejected, because the apparent relationship would depend completely on how we scaled the two axes.
  • Extending to 2026. Rejected after we found the orbit drift.

Limits

A drop in NDVI is a sign of clearing, not proof, since fire and drought lower it too. Squares are 5 km wide, so small clearings are diluted and our cleared area is a lower bound. Values arrive from GIBS already rounded into colour-map bins (0.6 °C for temperature). The temperature is that of the surface at about 10:30 in the morning, not the air temperature a weather station would report. And this is observational data, so while it's consistent with clearing causing the warming, it doesn't prove it.

Development process

How the work was split

  • Ethan Lam wrote the first exploration notebook, including the code that turns GIBS images back into numbers, which everything after it reuses.
  • Silas Jude made the global exploratory figures, set up the repository and GitHub Pages, and built the version with linked maps and the bar-chart year slider.
  • McAllister Blair wrote the deforestation notebook that narrowed the topic to the Amazon, and built “The Heat Left Behind”, which is the base of the final page.
  • Rami Abukhater built the third version and merged the three into the final submission.

Time spent

About 18 people-hours in total: roughly 5 hours each for Silas, Ethan and McAllister, and 3 hours for Rami.

What took the most time

Most of the time went into decoding GIBS images, finding out that temperatures after 2022 are not comparable, and choosing thresholds so that the control box shows almost no clearing. In the page itself, the slow parts were keeping 240,000 squares redrawing smoothly as the year changes (the map is a canvas, and only the overlays are SVG), and then reconciling three finished designs into one and cutting it back to a single effective graphic.
